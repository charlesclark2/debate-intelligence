# Academic case: candidate claims and sources

Status: **awaiting Charlie's approval.** Nothing here goes on the site until he strikes what he
does not want. `v1-e36-t09` builds the home page's academic band from the approved set and from
nothing else; its spec forbids a statistic that was estimated, rounded or recalled rather than read
off the source.

Claims come from the speaker notes of `Intro to WFB Debate.pptx`, verified against the published
sources, plus one study found while checking them.

## The audience problem, stated plainly

Nearly all of this literature studies large urban districts, and in every study that reports the
breakdown, the largest effects land on the lowest-achieving students. Whitefish Bay is an affluent
suburban district where college attendance is close to universal and the live question is not
whether a student goes but where. Two consequences:

1. **Headline effect sizes overstate what a Whitefish Bay family should expect.** Where a study
   reports effects by baseline achievement, the page quotes the top-quartile figure, not the
   average.
2. **College-access findings do not transfer and are not used.** Graduation rates, dropout rates and
   first-time college enrolment answer a question this community is not asking, and quoting them
   reads as either irrelevant or condescending. Charlie has ruled this category out by name and the
   ruling holds here.

## Recommended — use these

### 1. Debate helps students who are already doing well, not only students who are behind

> Among Boston public school students, taking part in policy debate raised English Language Arts
> achievement by 0.096 standard deviations for students already in the highest-performing quarter of
> their year group. The gains were concentrated in reading that requires analysis and argument
> rather than in language conventions.

- Schueler, B. E., & Larned, K. E. (2025). Interscholastic Policy Debate Promotes Critical Thinking
  and College-Going: Evidence From Boston Public Schools. *Educational Evaluation and Policy
  Analysis*. (Published online October 2023.)
- 3,515 debate participants, Boston Public Schools, 2007-08 to 2016-17.
- **Strongest design in this literature.** Student fixed effects — each student compared with
  themselves in years they did and did not debate — plus difference-in-differences. This is much
  closer to a causal estimate than the matched-comparison studies below.
- Headline effect is 0.13 SD overall (68% of a year of ninth-grade learning); lowest quartile 0.235
  SD; **highest quartile 0.096 SD**. Reading 0.101 SD; language 0.057 SD and not significant.
- Why this one leads: it is the only study that answers the question a Whitefish Bay parent is
  actually asking — does this do anything for a student who is already succeeding? — and the answer
  is yes, at about a tenth of a standard deviation, in exactly the analytical skills the page is
  claiming.
- Note: the AERA press release says 2007-08 to 2017-18; the working paper says 2016-17. Use the
  published paper's years.

### 2. Test scores and grade point average

> In a study of 35,788 Houston high school students, those who debated scored 109 points higher on
> the SAT and carried a grade point average 0.66 points higher than comparable students who did not.

- Ko, T. M., & Mezuk, B. (2021). Debate Participation and Academic Achievement among High School
  Students in the Houston Independent School District: 2012-2015. *Educational Research and
  Reviews*, 16(6), 219-225.
- 35,788 students, of whom 1,145 debated. Propensity score methods using sociodemographic
  characteristics and 8th-grade standardized test scores.
- GPA +0.66 (95% CI 0.64-0.68). SAT Math +52.43 (50.47-54.38). SAT Reading/Writing +57.05
  (55.14-58.96). The two SAT sections sum to 109.48, which is how a total score is reported.
- **Caveat that must appear on the page:** this is an association between debating and doing well,
  not proof that debating caused it. The researchers compared debaters with similar non-debaters
  statistically; they did not assign students to debate. Saying so is the honest move and it also
  models the thing the page is selling.
- No breakdown by baseline achievement is reported, so the audience caveat above applies and cannot
  be corrected for.

## Optional — defensible, weaker

### 3. High-ability students specifically

- Walker, A., & Kettler, T. (2020). Developing Critical Thinking Skills in High Ability Adolescents:
  Effects of a Debate and Argument Analysis Curriculum. *Talent*, 10(1), 21-39.
- Cornell Critical Thinking Test Level X, d = 0.40; argument analysis assessment, d = 0.41.
- The population is the right one — identified gifted and talented students rather than an urban
  district average — which is why it is here at all.
- **But the design is one-group pre-test/post-test with no control group.** Students improved over a
  course; some of that is a year of growing up. If it goes on the page it says "students in a gifted
  programme who took a debate and argument-analysis course improved on a standard critical-thinking
  test," and it does not carry a causal verb.
- Recommendation: use only if a second claim is wanted. One well-sourced claim beats two where the
  second invites the question.

## Recommended against — do not use

### 4. "Debaters are 22-30% more likely to be accepted at top-tier colleges"

**Exclude.** This is the most persuasive sentence available for this audience and the least
defensible one in the file. It traces to Luong (*Rostrum*, 2000), resting on a single 1999 *Wall
Street Journal* analysis, and is not peer reviewed. Charlie's own speaker notes already say to keep
it off a slide. A website carrying the school's name, published under an approved district policy,
is a stronger reason than a slide: in a community where families research what they are told, one
unverifiable admissions statistic discredits the two good claims sitting next to it.

### 5. College matriculation and completion

**Exclude.** Shackelford, Ratliff & Mezuk (2019, *Educational Research and Reviews*, 14(11),
397-409) found debaters in Chicago matriculated to four-year institutions at 63.5% against 47.7%,
but 70% of that effect ran through ACT score, the overall matriculation advantage disappeared once
ACT was controlled for, and debate was **not** associated with a higher likelihood of graduating
from college. In a district where nearly every student enrols somewhere, the finding measures a gap
that does not exist here, and the completion null is the part a sceptical reader would find first.

### 6. Graduation and dropout findings

**Exclude**, per Charlie's ruling and the audience problem above. For the record: Schueler & Larned
found debate raised graduation by 12 percentage points from a comparison mean of 68%. That baseline
is the reason it does not belong on this page.

### 7. Allen, Berkowitz, Hunt & Louden (1999) critical-thinking meta-analysis

**Exclude for now.** The finding is real and often cited — competitive forensics produced the
largest critical-thinking gains among the communication activities reviewed — but the paper sits
behind a paywall that refused every attempt to read it, so the effect size has not been read off the
source. Under t09's constraints a number nobody has read cannot go on the page. If Charlie can get
the PDF through the university library it is worth revisiting.

### 8. Mezuk et al. (2011), about +1 point on ACT Reading and ACT English

**Exclude.** Same reason: the figure comes from the deck's speaker notes and could not be verified
against the published paper. It is also largely superseded by the same group's 2021 Houston study,
which is larger, more recent and verified above. Dropping it costs the page nothing.

## What the page should not try to do with this

The four qualitative claims in the existing `whatDebateBuilds` band — research, speaking under
pressure, arguing both sides, a team that travels — stay as they are and gain no citations. They are
the honest version of what a season is like, and attaching research to them would turn a true
description into an overclaim. v1-e36-t09's spec already draws that boundary; this file does not
move it.
